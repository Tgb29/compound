Almost every story in the daily brief sits somewhere on one path. Once you know the path, the news gets much easier to place.

## 1. Discovery

Scientists pick a **target** — usually a protein involved in a disease — and look for a molecule that changes what it does. That molecule might be a **small molecule** (a chemical that can often be a pill) or a **biologic** (a large molecule made by living cells, like an antibody or a peptide, usually injected). This stage is where AI is starting to speed things up; see [Where AI actually helps](../ai-in-medicine/).

## 2. Preclinical testing

The candidate is tested in cells and animals to see whether it's safe enough to try in people. If it is, the company files an **IND** (Investigational New Drug application) with the FDA to start human trials.

## 3. Clinical trials

| Phase | Who | How many | The question |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Phase 1 | Usually healthy volunteers | 20–100 | Is it safe? What dose? |
| Phase 2 | People with the disease | A few hundred | Does it seem to work? |
| Phase 3 | People with the disease | Hundreds to thousands | Does it work better than placebo or the current treatment? |

Each trial names a **primary endpoint** in advance — the main thing it measures, like weight lost after 72 weeks. "Met its primary endpoint" means it passed. **Topline results** are the first headline numbers, usually in a press release, before the full data appears in a paper or at a medical conference.

**Phase 2 is where most drugs fail.** Across the industry, only about 1 in 10 drugs that enter Phase 1 end up approved.

## 4. Filing and the FDA decision

After Phase 3, the company files for approval: an **NDA** for small molecules, a **BLA** for biologics. The FDA commits to a decision deadline called the **PDUFA date** — about 10 months after it accepts a standard filing, about 6 for a priority review.

The answer is either approval or a **Complete Response Letter (CRL)**, which is a rejection listing what the company has to fix.

The FDA also has speed lanes — **Fast Track**, **Breakthrough Therapy**, **Priority Review** and **Accelerated Approval** — for drugs that treat serious diseases with few options.

## 5. After approval

Approval for one disease isn't approval for all of them. Companies run more trials to add new uses (the GLP-1 drugs went from diabetes to obesity to heart disease to sleep apnea this way). Safety monitoring continues for as long as the drug is sold.

## Why this matters for the news

- **Time:** 10–15 years from discovery to approval is normal.
- **Money:** once you count all the failures, estimates of the cost per approved drug run from about $1 billion to over $2 billion.
- **Catalysts:** trial readouts and PDUFA dates are the scheduled events that move biotech stocks. When a brief says "readout expected in Q1," this is the step it means.
