Age is the biggest risk factor for heart disease, most cancers, dementia and diabetes. The longevity field's bet is simple to state: instead of fighting those diseases one at a time, slow the process underneath all of them.

## Lifespan vs. healthspan

**Lifespan** is how long you live. **Healthspan** is how long you live in good health. Most serious researchers care more about the second — adding healthy years, not frail ones.

## What aging is, biologically

In 2013 a group of scientists published the **hallmarks of aging**, a list of the processes that go wrong in cells as we get older. The 2023 update has twelve, including:

- **Genomic instability** — DNA damage builds up.
- **Epigenetic alterations** — the chemical tags that control which genes are switched on drift out of place.
- **Cellular senescence** — damaged "zombie" cells stop dividing but don't die, and send out inflammatory signals.
- **Mitochondrial dysfunction** — cells' power plants work less well.
- **Deregulated nutrient sensing** — the pathways that respond to food (like **mTOR**) get out of balance.
- **Chronic inflammation** — the immune system runs hot all the time.

Most longevity companies pick one hallmark and try to fix it.

## What works in animals

- **Eating less.** Calorie restriction extends life in many animals, from worms to mice.
- **Rapamycin.** A drug that dials down mTOR. In the US government's mouse testing program, it extended lifespan even when started late in life — one of the most repeated results in the field.
- **Senolytics.** Drugs that clear senescent cells improved several age-related problems in mice. Human results so far are mixed.
- **Partial reprogramming.** In 2006 Shinya Yamanaka found four genes that can turn an adult cell back into a stem cell (he won a Nobel Prize for it). Briefly switching on those genes seems to make cells act younger without erasing what kind of cell they are. This is the idea behind some of the best-funded companies in the field.

## What's being tried in people

The FDA doesn't recognize aging itself as a disease, so no drug can be approved "for aging." Companies instead test their drugs on a specific age-related disease — an eye disease, muscle loss, a lung condition — and hope to broaden later.

A few things to know:

- **Metformin**, a cheap diabetes drug, is the subject of a proposed trial called **TAME** meant to show that a drug can delay several age-related diseases at once.
- **GLP-1 drugs** may turn out to be the first broad "healthspan" medicines by accident: they cut heart attacks and kidney damage, not just weight. See [GLP-1s and what comes next](../glp1s-and-whats-next/).
- **Dogs are going first.** Loyal is developing drugs to extend the lifespan of dogs, which age faster than people, so results come sooner. Check the [watchlist](../../watchlist/) for where it stands.

## The money

Longevity went from fringe to well-funded in the early 2020s: Altos Labs launched with about $3 billion, Sam Altman put $180 million into Retro Biosciences, and Coinbase co-founder Brian Armstrong backs NewLimit. Their progress and new rounds show up in the briefs.

## How to read longevity headlines

- **Is it mice or people?** Most "reverses aging" headlines are mouse studies.
- **Lifespan or a marker?** **Epigenetic clocks** estimate biological age from DNA tags. They're useful research tools but not yet proof that someone will live longer.
- **Who's selling what?** Supplements and clinics often cite real science for products that were never tested.
- **Healthspan or lifespan?** Feeling better at 70 is a real win, even if nobody lives to 150.
