"AI will cure disease" is a headline. What's actually happening is more specific, and more interesting.

## 1. Understanding biology

Proteins do most of the work in your body, and their 3D shape decides what they do. Working out a single protein's shape used to take years of lab work.

In 2020, Google DeepMind's **AlphaFold** showed it could predict protein shapes from their genetic sequence with close to lab accuracy. Its public database now holds predicted structures for over 200 million proteins. In 2024 Demis Hassabis and John Jumper (AlphaFold) shared the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/2024/summary/) with David Baker, whose lab uses AI to **design** brand-new proteins that don't exist in nature.

Newer models go further: predicting how proteins interact with DNA, RNA and drug molecules, and "language models" trained on DNA and protein sequences that can suggest new ones.

## 2. Finding and designing drugs

AI is used at several steps:

- **Picking targets** — sifting genetic and patient data for the protein most worth hitting.
- **Designing molecules** — generating and scoring millions of candidates on a computer before anyone makes one in a lab.
- **Predicting problems early** — flagging molecules likely to be toxic or poorly absorbed.

Well-known players: **Isomorphic Labs** (spun out of DeepMind), **Recursion**, **Insilico Medicine**, **Xaira**, **Chai Discovery** and **Generate:Biomedicines**. Big pharma companies sign partnerships with most of them. See the [watchlist](../../watchlist/) for the furthest-along AI-discovered drugs.

## 3. In the clinic

This is where AI already touches patients:

- **Reading scans.** The FDA has authorized well over a thousand AI-enabled medical devices, most of them in radiology.
- **Paperwork.** "Ambient scribes" listen to a doctor's visit and write the notes, one of the fastest-adopted AI tools in hospitals.
- **Answering questions.** Doctors increasingly use AI tools to search the medical literature during care.

## The bottleneck AI hasn't fixed

AI makes the **early** steps faster — some companies now go from idea to a drug ready for human testing in a year or two instead of four or five. But the clinical trials that follow still take years, and that's where most drugs fail (see [How a new medicine gets made](../how-a-medicine-gets-made/)). An AI-designed molecule still has to work in people.

So far, no drug discovered mainly by AI has reached full approval. The first ones in mid- and late-stage trials are the ones to watch.

## How to read AI-bio news

- **Which step did AI do?** Target, molecule, or trial design? "AI-discovered" can mean very different things.
- **Is there human data?** A molecule that worked in a computer and a mouse is still early.
- **Upfront or "biobucks"?** A "$1 billion AI partnership" is often mostly money that's only paid if things work. See [How to read biotech like an investor](../read-biotech-like-an-investor/).
