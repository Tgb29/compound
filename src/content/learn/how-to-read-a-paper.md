Most science news is a summary of a summary. Going to the source — even for ten minutes — is the fastest way to get better at this.

## Journal or preprint?

- **Journal papers** (Nature, Science, Cell, NEJM, The Lancet…) went through **peer review**: other scientists checked them before publication. That catches a lot, not everything.
- **Preprints** on [bioRxiv](https://www.biorxiv.org) and [medRxiv](https://www.medrxiv.org) are posted before peer review. They're how new results spread fast. Treat them as promising but unchecked.

## The 10-minute routine

1. **Title and abstract (2 min).** What question did they ask, and what's the one-sentence answer?
2. **Figure 1 (2 min).** The first figure usually shows the setup or the main result. Read the caption slowly.
3. **Who or what was studied? (2 min).** Cells in a dish, mice, or people? How many? Results in mice often don't hold up in humans.
4. **How big was the effect? (2 min).** Look for actual numbers, not just "significant." A drug that cuts risk "by half" may move it from 2% to 1%.
5. **Limitations and funding (2 min).** Near the end, authors list what their study can't show. Funding and conflicts of interest are listed too.

## Red flags in coverage

- **Mouse result, human headline.** "Scientists reverse aging" often means one marker changed in old mice.
- **"Linked to" or "associated with."** These come from observational studies, which can't prove one thing causes another. Only randomized trials can.
- **Surrogate endpoints.** A drug lowered a blood marker. Did it also help people live longer or feel better? Those aren't the same.
- **Relative numbers only.** Always ask "out of how many?"
- **Tiny trials.** A 12-person study is a hint, not an answer.

## The gold standard

A **randomized controlled trial (RCT)** assigns people to the drug or a placebo by chance, and in a **double-blind** trial neither the patients nor the doctors know who got which. That's the design the FDA usually needs for approval.

## Useful tools

- [PubMed](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov) — search almost every biomedical paper.
- [ClinicalTrials.gov](https://clinicaltrials.gov) — look up any trial's design, size and endpoints before the results are out.
- AI assistants are good at explaining a dense paragraph. Paste the paragraph, ask what it means, then check the answer against the paper itself.
