Biotech is one of the few parts of the market where a single press release can double a company's value or cut it in half. Here's how to make sense of the money stories. (None of this is investment advice — it's vocabulary.)

## Big pharma vs. biotech

**Big pharma** companies — Eli Lilly, Novo Nordisk, Pfizer, Merck — sell approved drugs and make billions in profit. **Biotechs** are usually smaller, often have no product yet, and spend investors' money to get one through trials. Pharma has cash and needs new drugs; biotechs have new drugs and need cash. Most deals come from that.

## What moves a biotech stock

- **Catalysts:** scheduled events like a trial readout, a PDUFA date or a big medical conference. Before one, the stock trades on hope; after it, on the result.
- **Binary events:** a catalyst where the answer is close to yes-or-no — a pass could send a small biotech up 100%, a fail down 70%.
- **Cash runway:** how many months of cash a company has left at its current spending. Short runway means it will probably raise money soon.
- **Dilution:** selling new shares to raise money. Each existing share then owns a smaller slice, so the price often drops on the news.

## Reading deal headlines

- **Acquisitions (M&A):** a bigger company buys a biotech outright, usually at a **premium** — well above the current share price — because it's paying for future sales.
- **Licensing deals:** a company buys the rights to one drug. The headline number is usually inflated by **milestones** (nicknamed "biobucks") that only get paid if the drug succeeds. The **upfront** payment is the real cash on day one. A "$2 billion deal" with $50 million upfront is a modest bet.
- **Royalties:** a cut of future sales, paid to whoever invented the drug.

## Why big pharma keeps shopping

Drugs are protected by patents for a limited time. When a blockbuster loses protection — the **patent cliff** — cheaper generic or biosimilar copies arrive and sales collapse. Several of the industry's biggest drugs lose US protection in the late 2020s (Merck's cancer drug Keytruda is the best-known example), so pharma companies buy pipelines to replace that revenue.

A growing share of the drugs they license now come from **Chinese biotechs**, which can move molecules into trials quickly and cheaply. You'll see that pattern a lot in the deals section.

## The market in one number

The **XBI** is an exchange-traded fund that holds hundreds of mostly small and mid-sized US biotechs in roughly equal weights, so it's the usual shorthand for "how is biotech doing." The **IBB** is a similar fund weighted toward the biggest companies. Biotech funding tends to move in waves with the XBI: when it's up, IPOs and big venture rounds follow.

## Comparing trial results

Headlines love to say drug A "beat" drug B. Unless they were tested in the same trial, be careful: different trials enroll different patients, run for different lengths of time and measure things slightly differently. Head-to-head trials are the only clean comparison.
